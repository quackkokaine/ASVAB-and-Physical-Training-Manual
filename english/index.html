Overview and study planTwo ASVAB sections test your English, and they carry real weight: your Word Knowledge and Paragraph Comprehension scores combine into Verbal Expression, which is counted twice in your AFQT score.

SectionWhat it testsComputer (CAT-ASVAB)Paper-and-pencil
Word Knowledge (WK)Word meanings: synonyms, words in a short sentence, word parts15 questions, 9 min35 questions, 11 min
Paragraph Comprehension (PC)Reading short passages: main idea, details, inferences, vocabulary in context, purpose and tone10 questions, 27 min15 questions, 13 min

*Computer figures are the scored questions listed on the official ASVAB site; the computer test can add unscored tryout questions, which lengthens the time limit. Paper figures are the commonly published ones. Confirm your version with your recruiter.

Your AFQT is built from AR + MK + 2 × Verbal Expression, where Verbal Expression combines WK and PC. That means English counts for about half of the score that decides whether you qualify to enlist.

How to use this guide

  • Word Knowledge comes first. Learn the strategies, then word parts, then the word lists. Do the practice box after each section.
  • Paragraph Comprehension is a skill, not a list. Learn the question types, study the worked passage, and practice reading short passages every day.
  • Use the Need more practice questions? box at the end of any section as many times as you like. Every question is new and random.
  • Finish with the practice set and the test-day strategy.

Word KnowledgeWK asks what a word means. The questions are quick: some show only the word, others put it in a short sentence. Choose the answer closest in meaning.

Word Knowledge strategies Seven tools

You will not know every word. These tools help you get the right answer even when a word is new. Read each card, then follow the example step by step.

1Use the sentence

Use it when: the word appears inside a sentence.

How it works: Read the whole sentence and look for clues: words about the situation, the feelings, or an opposite. Predict the meaning in your own words before you look at the choices.

Example
Problem: The storm began to abate by morning, and the sailors could finally rest.
Step 1: look at the clues: "by morning" and "finally rest".
Step 2: predict: the storm was calming down.
Step 3: choose the closest match: subside (grow less intense).

If a clue starts with "unlike", "but" or "however", the word probably means the opposite of the other idea.

2Break the word into parts

Use it when: you do not know the word, but you recognize a prefix, root or suffix.

How it works: Split the word into pieces, find the meaning of each piece, and put them back together.

Example
Problem: unpredictable.
Step 1: un- means "not". predict means "tell beforehand". -able means "able to be".
Step 2: put it together: "not able to be told beforehand".
Step 3: it means cannot be foreseen, so a match is "erratic".

The Prefixes, suffixes and roots section has the pieces you need most.

3Check whether it is positive or negative

Use it when: you know only the feeling of the word, not the exact meaning.

How it works: Decide if the word feels good or bad. Then cross out every choice with the opposite feeling.

Example
Problem: benevolent. Choices: cruel, charitable, hostile, indifferent.
Step 1: the root bene means "good", so the word is positive.
Step 2: cross out cruel, hostile and indifferent: they are negative or neutral.
Step 3: the answer is charitable.
4Eliminate, then guess

Use it when: you are stuck between choices or do not know any of them.

How it works: Cross out any choice you are sure is wrong. Even removing one improves your odds. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so always answer.

Example
Problem: deplete. Choices: exhaust, add, repair, praise.
Step 1: "add" and "praise" do not fit the sound or feeling of the word.
Step 2: "de-" suggests down or away, so "add" is the opposite.
Step 3: "exhaust" (use up) fits best.
5Think about other meanings

Use it when: none of the choices matches the first meaning you thought of.

How it works: Many words have more than one meaning. If the common meaning does not fit, try the less common one.

Example
Problem: The coach took a firm stand. Choices: company, unyielding, soft, loud.
Step 1: the common meaning of "firm" is a company, but "took a stand" does not fit.
Step 2: another meaning is "strong and steady".
Step 3: the answer is unyielding.
6Watch for look-alike traps

Use it when: a choice looks or sounds like the word.

How it works: A choice that sounds similar is often the trap. Match the meaning, not the sound.

Example
Problem: elicit. Choices: illegal, draw out, forbid, hide.
Step 1: "illegal" sounds like "elicit" (the word illicit), but that is a different word.
Step 2: elicit means to bring out, as in "elicit a response".
Step 3: the answer is draw out.
7Plug in each choice

Use it when: a sentence is given with the word underlined.

How it works: Replace the word with each choice and read the sentence. The one that keeps the same meaning is the answer.

Example
Problem: Her candid answer surprised us.
Step 1: try "secretive": Her secretive answer surprised us. It changes the meaning.
Step 2: try "frank": Her frank answer surprised us. It works.
Step 3: the answer is frank.

Context clues Read the sentence

Writers often leave hints about a word's meaning in the same sentence. Learn the five kinds of clues and you can often solve a question without knowing the word.

Clue typeWhat to look forExample
DefinitionThe sentence tells you what the word means, often after a comma or "which is".The cartographer, a person who makes maps, studied the coastline.
SynonymA word with a similar meaning is nearby.The plan was feasible, or possible, with the supplies we had.
Antonym (opposite)Words like "but", "unlike" or "however" point to an opposite.Unlike her gregarious brother, Sam was shy.
ExampleThe sentence gives examples of the word.The drill used ordnance, such as shells and rounds.
InferenceThe overall situation shows the meaning.The road was treacherous, so drivers crawled along.

Worked example

The speaker was so verbose that a five-minute point turned into a half-hour talk.
What does verbose mean?
Step 1: a short point became a long talk.
Step 2: the cause is "so verbose", so the word means using too many words.
Step 3: the answer is wordy.
  • Predict the meaning before you read the choices. It keeps you from being pulled by a tempting wrong answer.
  • Two choices that mean the same thing cannot both be correct, so neither of them is usually the answer.
  • If the sentence is about a person, check whether the word is positive or negative for that person.

Build a word

Try this: contradict
contra- (against) + dict (say) = to say against, so contradict means to say the opposite.
Try this: inaudible
in- (not) + aud (hear) + -ible (able to be) = not able to be heard.
Try this: transport
trans- (across) + port (carry) = to carry across.

Careful: a piece can mislead. The prefix in interest is not "between" and the letters in mist are not "wrong". Use parts as a clue, and check them against the choices.

Easily confused words Look-alikes

Some words sound alike or look alike and are often mixed up. Know the difference and you avoid easy traps on both sections.

WordsThe differenceIn a sentence
accept / exceptAccept means to receive. Except means other than.Everyone accepted the offer except Dana.
affect / effectAffect is usually a verb (to influence). Effect is usually a noun (a result).The weather will affect our plans; the effect will be a delay.
advice / adviseAdvice is a noun (a suggestion). Advise is a verb (to give advice).I will advise you, but you may ignore my advice.
complement / complimentComplement completes something. Compliment is praise.The sauce was a perfect complement to the meal, and she paid the cook a compliment.
principal / principlePrincipal means main (or a school head). Principle is a rule or belief.The principal reason was a matter of principle.
then / thanThen is about time. Than compares.First stretch, then run. She runs faster than I do.
lose / looseLose means to misplace or not win. Loose means not tight.Do not lose the loose screw.
stationary / stationeryStationary means not moving. Stationery is writing paper.The ship stayed stationary while I wrote on my stationery.
capital / capitolCapital is a city or money. Capitol is a building where lawmakers meet.Lawmakers met in the capitol in the state capital.
elicit / illicitElicit means to draw out. Illicit means illegal.The detective tried to elicit a confession about the illicit trade.
its / it'sIts shows possession. It's means "it is" or "it has".It's clear the dog hurt its paw.
farther / furtherFarther is physical distance. Further is degree or additional.We walked farther than planned and needed further rest.
  • If you cannot tell two words apart, think of a memory trick. Affect starts with "a" for action. Effect starts with "e" for end result.
  • Than compares and then is about time. Both have an "a" and an "e": the "a" is in than and compare.

Paragraph ComprehensionPC checks whether you understand what you read. Each question follows a short passage. Everything you need is in the passage, so use it.

Paragraph Comprehension strategies Eight tools

Strong readers do the same few things every time. Practice them until they are automatic.

1Preview the question first

Use it when: you have a passage and a question below it.

How it works: Read the question before the passage. It tells you what to look for, so you read with a purpose. Do not read the answer choices yet.

Example
Question: "According to the passage, why do ships rust faster?"
Step 1: you now know to look for a reason that is about ships.
Step 2: read the passage and watch for "ships" and a cause word such as "because".
Step 3: you find "Because salt in the water speeds up this reaction". Look for a choice that says this.
2Find the main idea

Use it when: a question asks what the passage is mostly about, or the best title.

How it works: Ask: what is the one big point? Check the first and last sentences. The correct answer covers the whole passage, not just one detail.

Example
Passage: sleep helps memory, with details about the brain and student recall.
Step 1: the first sentences set up a debate; the last sentence says rest is part of studying.
Step 2: say the main idea in one sentence: "Sleep helps the brain store what was learned."
Step 3: cross out choices that are too narrow (one detail) or too broad (claims more than the passage).

Wrong choices for main idea questions are often true details that are too narrow.

3Locate the detail

Use it when: the question asks "according to the passage" or "which of the following is stated".

How it works: Do not answer from memory. Go back to the passage, find the sentence that matches the question, and choose the answer that says the same thing in other words.

Example
Question: "What does the brain do during sleep?"
Step 1: scan for "sleep" and "brain".
Step 2: the passage says the brain "replays what we learned" and "moves important details into long-term memory".
Step 3: choose the answer that restates this, even with different words.
4Make a supported inference

Use it when: the question says "infer", "suggests", "most likely" or "implies".

How it works: An inference is a conclusion the passage supports without saying it directly. Find the clues, combine them, and choose the answer the text supports. Do not use outside knowledge.

Example
Passage: Marcus gripped the wall and stayed in the shallow end at his first lesson.
Step 1: what do these actions show?
Step 2: he was uneasy about the water.
Step 3: the best inference is "He was nervous". "He was an expert" has no support.
5Figure out words in context

Use it when: the question asks what a word means "as used in the passage".

How it works: Cover the word, read the sentence, and think of your own word that fits. Then test each choice by plugging it in.

Example
Sentence: The recruits were reluctant at first, worried about making mistakes.
Step 1: the clue is "worried about making mistakes".
Step 2: someone who worries will hold back, so the word means unwilling.
Step 3: choose unwilling.
6Think about purpose and tone

Use it when: the question asks why the author wrote it, or what the attitude is.

How it works: Purpose is usually to inform, explain, persuade or entertain. Tone is the feeling in the writing: look at word choice and whether the author argues for something.

Example
Passage: asks the town council to extend library hours.
Step 1: the author gives reasons and ends with "The council should vote".
Step 2: the author wants action, so the purpose is to persuade.
Step 3: the tone is persuasive and concerned, not humorous.
7Beware extreme words

Use it when: a choice contains "always", "never", "all", "only" or "completely".

How it works: The passage rarely supports such strong claims. Be suspicious unless the passage says exactly that.

Example
Choices: "Tornadoes are always larger than hurricanes."
Step 1: "always" is a strong word, so check the passage.
Step 2: the passage says tornadoes are smaller, so the choice is wrong anyway.
Step 3: moderate choices like "typically" are often safer.
8Pace yourself

Use it when: you feel pressed for time.

How it works: Know your time per question and keep moving. On the computer test you cannot go back, so answer each one before you move on. On paper, skip hard ones and return.

Example
Computer: 10 questions in 27 minutes is about 2.7 minutes each.
Paper: 15 questions in 13 minutes is about 52 seconds each.
Step 1: spend about a third of the time reading and the rest answering.
Step 2: do not reread the whole passage; go back to the sentence you need.

Question types Know what is asked

Almost every Paragraph Comprehension question falls into one of a few types. Name the type first, then use the matching approach.

TypeTypical wordingHow to answer
Main idea"The passage is mainly about…", "The best title is…"Find the big point. Check the first and last sentences. Reject details that are too narrow.
Detail"According to the passage…", "Which is stated…"Go back to the passage, find the matching sentence, and choose the paraphrase.
Inference"The author implies…", "most likely…", "can be concluded…"Use clues in the text. Choose the answer the passage supports, not one that is only possible.
Vocabulary in context"As used in the passage, the word … means…"Predict the meaning from the sentence, then plug in each choice.
Purpose"The author wrote this to…"Decide: inform, explain, persuade or entertain. Look at what the author asks the reader to do or know.
Tone"The tone of the passage is…"Look at word choice. Is the author neutral, concerned, humorous, critical or persuasive?
Sequence"What happens after…", "Which step comes first?"Look for order words: first, next, then, finally.
Cause and effect"Why did…", "What caused…"Look for words such as because, so, since and as a result.

A quick tip on tone words

  • Persuasive: argues for an action or opinion.
  • Informative: gives facts in a neutral voice.
  • Critical: points out problems.
  • Admiring: praises someone or something.
  • Humorous: tries to be funny.
  • Concerned: expresses worry or urgency.

Worked passage Step by step

Passage
Rust forms when iron is exposed to oxygen and moisture. First, water on the surface of the metal begins to dissolve a thin layer of iron. Next, oxygen reacts with the dissolved iron to form iron oxide, the reddish flaky substance we call rust. Because salt in the water speeds up this reaction, ships and coastal bridges rust faster than structures inland. Protective paint slows the process by keeping water and air away from the metal.

Question 1 (detail)

Why do ships rust faster than structures inland?

  • A. They are made of softer iron.
  • B. Salt in the water speeds up the reaction.
  • C. Paint attracts oxygen.
  • D. They are always wet.
Answer: B
The passage says "Because salt in the water speeds up this reaction, ships and coastal bridges rust faster". A and C are not mentioned. D uses "always", an extreme word, and the passage does not say it.

Question 2 (sequence)

What happens right after water begins to dissolve a thin layer of iron?

  • A. The iron turns to salt.
  • B. Paint seals the surface.
  • C. Oxygen reacts with the dissolved iron.
  • D. The metal becomes stronger.
Answer: C
The word "Next" shows the order: first the water dissolves iron, next oxygen reacts with it to form iron oxide.

Question 3 (inference)

Which can be concluded from the passage?

  • A. A painted ship near the coast will rust more slowly than an unpainted one.
  • B. Rust is harmless.
  • C. All metals rust.
  • D. Inland bridges never rust.
Answer: A
Paint keeps water and air away, which slows rusting, so a painted ship should rust more slowly. B has no support. C and D use extreme words ("All", "never").

Question 4 (vocabulary)

As used in the passage, dissolve most nearly means

  • A. break down into the liquid
  • B. harden
  • C. paint
  • D. hide
Answer: A
Water "begins to dissolve a thin layer of iron" and then oxygen reacts with the "dissolved iron". The iron breaks down into the water, so A fits.

Word Knowledge quick rules

  • If you know it, answer fast and save time for hard ones.
  • Use prefixes, roots and suffixes when the word is new.
  • Decide positive or negative, then cross out the opposite.
  • Plug each choice into the sentence.
  • Never leave a question blank on paper. Guess.

Paragraph Comprehension quick rules

  • Read the question first. Know what to look for.
  • Main idea: one sentence, not too narrow, not too broad.
  • Detail: go back and find the sentence.
  • Inference: only what the passage supports.
  • Watch for extreme words: always, never, all, only.
  • Check your choice against the passage one last time.

The week before

  • Review your list of missed words every day.
  • Read something for 15 minutes a day: news, a novel, a manual.
  • Sleep well the night before. A rested brain reads faster.
  • Bring the required ID. Eat something light before the test.
